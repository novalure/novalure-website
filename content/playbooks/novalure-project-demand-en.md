<section markdown="1">
<p class="kicker">Recognition</p>
<h2>Do these situations sound familiar?</h2>
<p class="lead">Developments rarely fail for lack of interest. They fail in what happens between interest and a buying conversation. Three moments from everyday project sales:</p>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 1 · Monday, 9:10 am</span>
<h4>“Which of these are serious?”</h4>
<p>Fourteen enquiries arrived over the weekend: portal, project page, campaign. Your sales team opens the list and asks the only question that matters — and nobody can answer it. So the calls start from the top, in order of arrival rather than readiness to buy.</p></div>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 2 · The campaign report</span>
<h4>“It’s working — cost per lead is down.”</h4>
<p>The report shows more enquiries at lower cost. At the same time, sales reports that quality has clearly dropped. Both are right — they are simply not measuring the same thing. The campaign is judged on clicks and lead prices before anyone has seen the quality of a single conversation.</p></div>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 3 · The first call</span>
<h4>Both sides start from zero.</h4>
<p>A prospective buyer downloaded the brochure days ago and asked for an appointment. On the call, he asks about floor plans he already has — and your salesperson asks about budget, timing and preferred unit, all for the first time. Two prepared people would have been discussing the buying decision.</p></div>

If at least one of these feels familiar, the problem is rarely the marketing and rarely the sales team. It is the path in between — and that path can be made visible.
</section>

<section markdown="1" class="pagejob">
<p class="kicker">Economic friction</p>
<h2>What the current way of working really costs</h2>
<p class="lead">These costs never appear on an invoice, yet they accrue every week — in time, priorities and missed conversations:</p>
<div class="cost">
<div class="item"><h4>Your most expensive people’s time</h4><p>Your sales team asks on the phone what a well-built enquiry path could have captured long before: intent, timing, budget fit, preferred unit. Selling time turns into sorting time.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4>The wrong priorities</h4><p>Without context, the loudest enquiry wins, not the most mature one. The undecided get quick call-backs while ready buyers wait — because both look identical in the same list.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="cost">
<div class="item"><h4>Decisions made blind</h4><p>Pricing, unit mix and media budget are steered without any insight flowing back from real conversations. The report shows clicks — not what buying decisions are currently failing on.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4>Opportunities that go quiet</h4><p>Enquiries without an owner and a dated next step simply sit there. The buyer who wanted to purchase “in six months” is never contacted again — and buys elsewhere.</p></div>
</div>

How large this effect is in money terms depends on project, market and price bracket — so this playbook deliberately quotes no invented figures. The direction is unambiguous: the most expensive minutes of any development are the ones your sales team spends in the wrong conversations — and the ones in which the right conversation never happens.
</section>

<section markdown="1" class="pagejob">
<p class="kicker">Leak map</p>
<h2>Where context gets lost along the way</h2>
<p class="lead">A project path has five stations. At every hand-off, the context a prepared buying conversation needs can break. This is the leak map:</p>
<div class="leakmap">
<div class="leakrow">
<div class="stage"><span class="n">01</span><span class="t">Project presence</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">02</span><span class="t">Enquiry</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">03</span><span class="t">Qualification</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">04</span><span class="t">Follow-up</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">05</span><span class="t">Handover</span></div>
</div>
<div class="leaks">
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 1→2</b><p>The trigger is lost: which asset, which campaign, which motive produced the enquiry? Without the source, the conversation has no starting point.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 2→3</b><p>The form collects contact details instead of buying context. Timing, budget fit and unit preference have to be asked for later, one call at a time.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 3→4</b><p>No owner, no date. Whether anyone follows up depends on who happens to remember — not on a defined next step.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 4→5</b><p>Sales receives a name and a number instead of a score, source, timing and next step. The first conversation starts from zero.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 5→1</b><p>Insights from conversations never flow back. Campaign and positioning keep being steered by clicks instead of by what buyers actually hesitate over.</p></div>
</div>
</div>

Every single station can be well made in itself — page, campaign, sales team. The leaks happen at the hand-offs, which is precisely why one more isolated measure rarely solves the problem.
</section>

<section markdown="1" class="pagejob">
<p class="kicker">Quality criteria</p>
<h2>What a prepared buying conversation must show</h2>
<p class="lead">“Qualified” is not a feeling — it is a minimum of context. Before your sales team picks up the phone, six things should be visible:</p>
<div class="criteria">
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">1</div><p><b>Source and trigger.</b> Where did the enquiry come from, and what set it off — brochure, project page, campaign, referral?</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">2</div><p><b>Unit interest.</b> Which unit type, which size, which position in the scheme? “Interested in the project” is too little for a good conversation.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">3</div><p><b>Timing.</b> Buying within zero to three months, three to six — or later? That decides priority and the goal of the call.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">4</div><p><b>Budget fit.</b> Does the price bracket broadly fit, is it open, or does it not fit? No credit check — an honest self-assessment is enough to prioritise.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">5</div><p><b>Status and open questions.</b> Which documents have been sent, which questions are outstanding? Nothing sent twice, nothing asked twice.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">6</div><p><b>A next step with a date and an owner.</b> Call-back, viewing, financing conversation — agreed, scheduled and assigned to one person.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="note">If several of these are routinely missing, that is not a sales problem — it is a handover problem. Your sales team can only prioritise as well as the path before it supplies context.</p>
</section>

<p class="kicker">Example</p>
<h2>How a project path changes</h2>
<p class="lead">This is what the difference looks like in practice — using the example of a new-build scheme of around forty units, with enquiries from portal, project page and campaign:</p>
<div class="case">
<div class="ph before"><b>Before</b><p>All enquiries land in one inbox. Sales works the list in order of arrival; viewing slots go to the undecided while ready buyers wait. The campaign is judged on cost per lead and scaled further.</p></div>
<div class="ph change"><b>Changed process</b><p>Buying context is captured at the point of enquiry: trigger, unit preference, timing, budget fit. Every enquiry gets an owner and a dated next step. Handover happens only with full context; once a week, sales feedback flows back into the campaign.</p></div>
<div class="ph after"><b>Result</b><p>Fewer, but prepared, conversations. Viewings happen with buying intent, priorities follow maturity instead of gut feeling — and unit mix, price arguments and media are steered by real conversations.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="evnote">Illustrative example to demonstrate the principle — no client figures and no promise of results. How much a project path improves depends on the project, market, offer and budget.</p>

The decisive point is the order of operations: first the path, then the reach. A path that holds context can process demand — a fragmented one merely converts it into additional work.
