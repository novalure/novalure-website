<section markdown="1">
<p class="kicker">Recognition</p>
<h2>Do these situations sound familiar?</h2>
<p class="lead">International demand feels like a win at first — and then becomes a stress test for every step of the path. Three typical moments:</p>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 1 · The translated campaign</span>
<h4>The enquiries arrive — in four languages.</h4>
<p>The advert was translated, the reach extended. Now enquiries in English, French and Dutch sit in the inbox — and are answered three days later in German, with a brochure that assumes the buyer already knows how purchasing works in the destination country.</p></div>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 2 · The process questions</span>
<h4>“How does this actually work in your country?”</h4>
<p>A buyer from abroad asks about the process, purchase costs, financing as a non-resident, and what can be signed from a distance. The sales team improvises the answers by email — new every time, different every time, and never with a document that builds trust.</p></div>
<div class="scene"><span class="tag">Situation 3 · Time-zone ping-pong</span>
<h4>Three emails, no conversation.</h4>
<p>Appointment suggestions cross each other across time zones; the buyer replies in the evening, the office in the morning. After two weeks and three polite emails the enquiry is cold — not from lack of interest, but because nobody owned the job of untangling it.</p></div>

None of these situations comes from bad work. They arise when international reach meets a path that was built for the home market.
</section>

<section markdown="1" class="pagejob">
<p class="kicker">Economic friction</p>
<h2>What the current way of working really costs</h2>
<p class="lead">Advertising internationally has become easy. Selling internationally has not. The costs arise in four places:</p>
<div class="cost">
<div class="item"><h4>Reach before process readiness</h4><p>Marketing scales visibility before the path behind it can process international enquiries. Every additional enquiry then produces effort rather than opportunity.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4>Ill-fitting enquiries at volume</h4><p>Without early context — intended use, budget range, time horizon — the curious and the unsuitable fill the list, and the few serious buyers disappear inside it.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="cost">
<div class="item"><h4>Missing trust signals</h4><p>International buyers decide at a distance. Without explained processes, fitting documents and reliable responses, trust migrates to whichever provider supplies both.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4>Follow-up without ownership</h4><p>Language, time zones and unclear responsibility turn interest into silence. The enquiry was real — the path lost it.</p></div>
</div>

How high this price is depends on the project, the target markets and the price bracket — there are no honest blanket figures for it, and this playbook invents none. The pattern, though, is reliable: international demand forgives no loose ends. Every gap that proximity papers over in the home market becomes visible at a distance — and expensive.
</section>

<p class="lead">The same five stations as at home — but at every hand-off, distance adds a fracture point of its own:</p>
<div class="leakmap">
<div class="leakrow">
<div class="stage"><span class="n">01</span><span class="t">Presence</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">02</span><span class="t">Enquiry</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">03</span><span class="t">Qualification</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">04</span><span class="t">Follow-up</span></div><div class="arrow">→</div>
<div class="stage"><span class="n">05</span><span class="t">Handover</span></div>
</div>
<div class="leaks">
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 1→2</b><p>Translated, not localised: the language is right, but the trust signals, process explanation and documents for the target market are missing.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 2→3</b><p>Intended use (own use, holiday, investment), financing country and time horizon are not captured — yet these are exactly what determine maturity.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 3→4</b><p>Decision windows stay unknown: visit trips and viewing dates set the rhythm of an international purchase — miss them and every call comes at the wrong time.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 4→5</b><p>Language, time zone and ownership are unresolved — follow-up becomes polite ping-pong without a result.</p></div>
<div class="leak"><b>Leak 5→1</b><p>The handover does not explain the local process: sales talks about the apartment while the buyer is still thinking about the procedure.</p></div>
</div>
</div>

The difference from the home market: there, proximity and shared context forgive many gaps. At a distance there is no such mercy — the buyer journey itself has to create the closeness.
</section>

<section markdown="1" class="pagejob">
<p class="kicker">Quality criteria</p>
<h2>What a prepared international conversation must show</h2>
<p class="lead">An international buyer conversation is prepared when six things are visible beforehand:</p>
<div class="criteria">
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">1</div><p><b>Language and preferred channel.</b> Which language will the conversation be in — and is video call, phone or email preferred?</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">2</div><p><b>Intended use.</b> Own use, holiday home or investment? Each intention changes the questions, the documents and the goal of the call.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">3</div><p><b>Financing arrangements.</b> From which country will the purchase be financed or paid — and is that framework already in motion? A self-assessment is enough; no checks.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">4</div><p><b>Decision window and travel dates.</b> When will the buyer be in the country, and when is the decision due? Visit trips set the tempo of international purchases.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">5</div><p><b>Process questions answered.</b> Procedure, purchase costs, required documents, remote signing: settled before the conversation turns to the property itself.</p></div>
<div class="crit"><div class="ic">6</div><p><b>A next step scheduled across time zones.</b> An agreed appointment that works for both sides — with one responsible person behind it.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="note">Without these points, every international conversation becomes a first contact — no matter how many emails were exchanged before it.</p>
</section>

<p class="kicker">Example</p>
<h2>How an international buyer journey changes</h2>
<p class="lead">This is how the flow changes — using the example of a holiday-property scheme addressing buyers from several countries:</p>
<div class="case">
<div class="ph before"><b>Before</b><p>The campaign runs, translated, in three markets. Enquiries arrive in several languages and are answered late and inconsistently; process questions are improvised email by email. Viewings fail on scheduling — the list grows, the conversations do not.</p></div>
<div class="ph change"><b>Changed process</b><p>The buyer journey is localised per target market: process and costs are explained; intended use, financing country and travel dates are captured at the enquiry. Every enquiry gets a language, a responsible person and a next step scheduled across time zones; handover happens with full context.</p></div>
<div class="ph after"><b>Result</b><p>Fewer, but fitting, international conversations. Visit trips become planned decision windows, sales talks about the property instead of the formalities — and reach is increased only once the path can process it.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="evnote">Illustrative example to demonstrate the principle — no client figures and no promise of results. Outcomes depend on the project, target markets, offer and budget.</p>

International buyers are not an extra channel to let “run alongside”. They are a buyer journey of their own — with their own questions, their own rhythm and their own way of building trust.
